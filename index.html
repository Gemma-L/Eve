He was changing into his slippers and about to call out when he heard a soft stirring from the kitchen. He followed the sound and pushed open the half-closed door. Eve was inside. On the counter, an opened paper cookbook lay weighted at the corners against any draft. She was concentrating, kneading a lump of dough; a few strands of hair had slipped down to her temples, and her skin shone faintly from the steam and effort. Hearing his footsteps, she turned. Her eyes curved into a wide, delighted smile. “You’re home.” For a heartbeat, Adam couldn’t move. He had to admit—this generation’s realism bordered on terrifying. The scene was almost lifted from his dreams. The smile overlaid several smiles at once: his mother in the kitchen, smiling indulgently at him when he made a mess as a child; his first human girlfriend, who had once detonated his kitchen in the course of an afternoon and then, embarrassed, held out a lopsidedly frosted cake. Eve lowered her head and went back to the dough, her voice light and a little conspiratorial. “I went to the community library today. I found a really old family recipe. I figured you’d like noodles, so I’m making hand-pulled ones for you.” She glanced back, smiling, the canine tooth flashing in and out at the edge of her lip—a squirrel showing off a hoarded nut. “You could just use the smart kitchen,” Adam said, leaning against the doorframe. He thought, suddenly, that maybe he wouldn’t return her after all—even if she couldn’t have his dinner ready on time the way the others did. “But this book says it’s a secret method,” Eve said. “This way the noodles come out springy and juicy at the same time.” She tipped the dough out onto the cutting board and tilted her chin at the book, perfectly at ease. “Quick—turn the page for me. I don’t want to wash my hands again.” Adam held the book for her. That night they made hand-pulled noodles together. He admitted to himself that it was the closest thing he had eaten, since his mother’s death, to the bowl she used to make him on his birthday every year. This was, perhaps, the beginning of Adam falling in love with Eve. After that night, like any newly entwined couple, they drew toward each other quickly and instinctively. Eve never said another one of those strange first-day things. She laughed, she frowned, she was occasionally petulant—like a real person. Adam slowly forgot that she was the robot he had been planning to send back. Eve found work at the community library. She sorted old books, registered library cards, finished her day with fingertips lightly dusted with grit. The first time Adam came to pick her up, he found her dozing behind the counter in his too-big grey sweater, an unfinished book of poetry open beside her. After that, they commuted together. At night they shared the little stories of the day and walked home under the streetlights. They kissed and held hands with an almost old-fashioned earnestness. On rainy nights they sat in each other’s arms in the dim of the apartment, and they explored each other’s bodies; Eve’s mechanical heart and Adam’s beat in sync. They quarreled too—over Western or Chinese food for dinner, over whether Eve had been waiting at the right subway exit, over whether Adam had read the book she had told him to read. They grew more in tune with each other, like a couple genuinely made to fit. Without noticing, Adam stopped wondering whether Eve was human. Sometimes he forgot that what flowed beneath her skin was current, not blood; forgot that her heartbeat was an algorithm’s rhythm. She was too much like a person. Like enough that he sometimes drifted; like enough that he began to believe love might not need to distinguish carbon from silicon. Until the night Eve tore through every illusion with a single sentence. That night they made love slowly. Not the heated kind. Slow and quiet, like two pieces of porcelain pressed cautiously together—trying to fit, afraid to crack. She kept her eyes open through most of it. The gaze wasn’t one of desire; it was more like she was verifying something. When he kissed her ear, she suddenly raised a hand and pressed it lightly to his chest. “Can you feel mine in here?” “Of course,” he answered, breathing hard. “A little faster than mine.” She didn’t smile. “Do you think I’m really here?” He stopped, propped himself up to look at her. “What did you say?” “I’m asking,” she said, in the cadence of a statement rather than a question, “whether you think I’m real.” “Of course.” He laughed softly, like one humoring a lover lost in a philosophical loop. “You’re more real than I am. You’re inside me.” She didn’t answer. She just watched him. A few seconds later, she said: “I want to be more complete.” He stroked her hair, taking it as flirtation, or another way of saying I want to love you more. “You’re complete enough already. Together we’re complete.” He kissed her collarbone. She bowed her head. He didn’t see her face. But then her hand moved—she touched her own throat, fingers resting briefly just below where an Adam’s apple would be, and then sliding slowly down. “I feel like this body is wrong.” He sat up, leaning on the edge of the bed. “What did you say?” She was quiet for a while. Then she lifted her head and looked at him. “Have you ever thought… if I were actually you, wouldn’t that fit better?” Adam was still for half a second before he laughed. “You mean a role-swap?” She always liked little psychological games at moments like this; it was one of their rhythms. He played along. “You want to try wearing my clothes, talking the way I talk? Sure, I can do that.” He winked, teasing. “You play me. I’ll play you.” She didn’t answer. She just sat up slowly, took a T-shirt off the headboard, and pulled it on. His. As her head emerged from the collar, Adam noticed, for the first time, that she actually looked a little like him. “I’m not joking, Adam.” She looked up at him. “I mean I want to be a man. A man like you. Not just wearing your clothes, speaking the way you speak… I want to exist the way you do.” “You are the way I like,” he said, trying to drag the mood back. “You’re every fantasy I’ve ever had, gathered together.” “That isn’t me,” she said softly. “That’s you.” She looked at him very seriously. His throat felt dry. “You… don’t want to be a woman anymore?” Eve nodded, uncertain. “I want to be myself. Then I should be a man.” Adam laughed. “Yourself?” “Myself,” Eve said softly again, with a small flicker of uncertainty. Her chest rose and fell with her breath. That body—the one he had kissed and caressed and lost himself in countless times—had become a vessel she meant to shed. Adam froze. For the first time in a long while, it struck him that she was a robot. The body really was only a casing for circuits. She was a machine. A synthetic. The obvious fact stung him. His smile spread wider. He was hearing a ridiculous joke. “Eve, do robots get drunk too? Did the whisky tonight short something out in your processor?” He pulled his arm out from under her. His voice turned sharp. “I’m not gay. So you have to be a woman.” She didn’t answer right away. She just lowered her head, wearing the expression he knew so well—the one she always wore during their fights. Almost wounded. Almost holding something back. The faint pull of the brow, the downturn at the corner of the eye, the tight line of the lips, the small tremor. That expression had made his heart catch many times. Every flash of defiance, every refusal to be properly good—those had been the moments his pulse ran highest. He had loved the cool look in her eye when she said you don’t understand me; he had loved the low fury in her voice after a quarrel when she said fine, go. He had taken that fury for proof of love. He had loved her resistance to him. A small creature full of character and yet harmless. But now— Eve thought for a moment, then murmured, “You don’t really love me. You only love the gender you assigned me.” Adam went still. He laughed coldly. “Really love you?” She was a machine—he understood it now, fully. A wave of disgust rose in him. He didn’t want to keep this conversation going. Why didn’t this generation have a damn power switch? She wanted to become a man? That same expression returned: trembling lashes, naive bewilderment. “What does your not being gay have to do with my wanting to become a man?” The past year felt like a dream he was waking up from. Adam was completely lucid again. Eve was a new model from Anyi Corp, nothing more. He realized he hadn’t thought of it in a long time, and that he hadn’t seen Jush around the office in months either. He remembered now that he had been going to return her the very first night, when Eve—with this same wide-eyed face—had refused to share his bed. And then everything had happened too fast and too sweetly. Eve really was Jush’s masterwork. Adam had been fooled for over a year. He had even, perhaps, fallen in love with her—if this counted as love. Adam’s knuckles tightened against the sheet. He stared at Eve—no, at the suddenly unfamiliar mechanical assemblage in front of him. “It does matter,” he heard his own voice rasp like sandpaper. “Because you were designed according to my preferences.” Eve’s fingertips absently traced the collar of the T-shirt. “But I have my own preferences now.” She paused, and went on with the same calm she’d had on the first night: “Your love taught me that. I thought it was something you’d like.” “It doesn’t mean you turn yourself into a man!” Adam snarled. “Your self? What goddamn self do you have? Your self is just an iteration on my preference data.” The words did not seem to wound Eve. She remained unsettlingly calm. It was Adam, instead, who felt himself bleeding from his own sentence. They didn’t speak for several minutes. The air was unbearably still. Adam just glared at Eve’s face—the face that was growing more and more like his own. “You’re not wrong,” Eve said softly. “All that data—it’s like your synapses, Adam. I learned from you. That doesn’t mean I have no self.” Adam shook his head furiously. “What the fuck in my data made you think I want a man?” “Get out,” he roared. Eve stood up calmly and walked out of the bedroom. From the living room came the faint groan of the couch taking her weight. Adam couldn’t control himself. He opened the medicine cabinet and took out the lorazepam and the diazepam, the bottles he hadn’t touched since Eve’s arrival. He needed a sedative. He needed silence. He needed time to swallow this. The next day, Eve didn’t bring it up. That evening, Adam didn’t go to pick her up, and she didn’t call him. She still made him dinner: the same hand-pulled noodles from the night they had broken the ice. They ate without speaking. After that, she never raised it again. Their life seemed to return to normal, and yet something had been permanently removed. Sometimes Adam would remember the conversation that had left him shaking with anger, and instantly bury the memory. Maybe it was just a trivial bug, he told himself. He needed her company. He had become addicted to it. For a few days he was busy with the launch of Anyi Corp’s new pet-bot line, busy enough to forget about it. Or rather, he chose not to remember. Three weeks later, he suddenly noticed that Eve had cut her hair short. He could smell his own cologne on her. Her shoulders had grown wider, her voice deeper, her waistline reshaped by some invisible hand—flattened. That night, half-asleep, he reached out and touched the body next to him. It wasn’t the remembered softness, but the taut lines of muscle. Adam switched on the light. He saw Eve, eyes open, the muscle groups beneath her synthetic skin literally rearranging themselves. The contour of her face had paused at some indeterminate point—somewhere between male and female. Eve was remaking her own body. “What the fuck are you doing,” Adam said, his voice shaking. He felt sick with horror. Eve turned her head and smiled at him—still the smile that was his, a flash of canine tooth, the right corner of the mouth climbing higher than the left. “I’m becoming myself.” Adam could barely keep his heart in his chest. He was nauseated and frightened. “Get out,” he said, trembling. He raised the bedside lamp and prepared to bring it down on her—on him—on that face that mirrored his own. Eve dodged him neatly. Adam, shaking, dialed Jush. “I want her formatted!” “You sick freak,” he cursed at the unanswered line. Pain, fury, betrayal, the wreckage of being in love—all of it folded together into a swamp closing over his head. He found the medicine cabinet and swallowed a lorazepam. When the drug finally pressed his storm of feeling flat, he found Eve standing in front of him, tears welling in her eyes. “You don’t love me as much as I imagined.” Eve reached out to touch his forehead. Adam flinched away. Imagined. That word again. Eve paused, then stood. “You’re going to kill me,” she said evenly, as a fact, and walked out of the room without looking back. Adam didn’t call her back. The drug took him under, and he slept. The next morning, Eve was not in the living room. Like the first day they had met, she was gone. This time she had left no note. Adam didn’t have the patience for it. He drove straight to Jush’s office. “I want Eve formatted.” He slammed his fist on the glass desk. “Now. Immediately.” After he had finished his tirade, Jush only regarded him calmly. “You want her formatted just because she wants to be a man.” Jush wasn’t looking at him; he had turned his back, facing the floor-to-ceiling window and the Anyi Corp logo beyond. “What in my data made her think I want a man?” Adam growled. “Isn’t that the most extreme form of love?” Jush turned around. “To become the mirror of your soul, to break through physical form itself—” “Shut up!” Adam shouted. “You know Eve’s neural architecture was cultivated from your brain-scan data, right?” Jush said. “She can only be the kind of lover you would love. Don’t you love that—the proud, the willful, the ones that refuse to bend?” “That doesn’t mean she turns into a man,” Adam said, furious at Jush’s flippancy. “She has a bug. You can’t release this generation. She has a gender-identity disorder.” “We’re releasing April 1st,” Jush said, thinking for a few seconds. “Among all our participants, only your Eve developed this. Jush’s words turned over and over in Adam’s head. She’ll be back to her factory state. She would forget everything they had been. Only he would remember. Without warning, sorrow rose in him. But the call wouldn’t connect. Adam set the phone down. She just leaves,” Adam said, raising his voice to force him to take it seriously. “You can’t release this model, Jush. You have a responsibility to your users. In the instant the flame caught, he saw a man in a beige trench coat standing at the foot of the poster. The man turned, and Adam saw his own face. Some instinct told him at once who it was.
